Complex potential#

In two-dimensional incompressible, irrotational flow, the flow can often be represented by a complex potential

\[\Omega(z) = \Phi(z) + i\Psi(z),\]

where \(z = x + iy\), \(\Phi\) is the velocity potential, and \(\Psi\) is the stream function.

The complex potential is an analytic function of \(z\) in the flow domain, so it satisfies the Cauchy-Riemann equations

\[\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial y}, \qquad \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial x}.\]

These equations imply that both \(\Phi\) and \(\Psi\) are harmonic, so they satisfy Laplace’s equation,

\[\nabla^2 \Phi = 0, \qquad \nabla^2 \Psi = 0.\]

In complex varaibles we can write Laplace’s equation as .. math:

\frac{\partial^2 \Omega}{\partial z \partial \bar{z}} = 0,

Note that since \(\Omega\) is analytic, it depends only on \(z\) and not on \(\bar{z}\). This is a key property of analytic functions, and it implies that the complex potential by definition satisfies Laplace’s equation in the flow domain.

Basic flow equations#

The complex velocity is obtained by differentiating the complex potential,

\[-\frac{d\Omega}{dz} = q_x + iq_y,\]

where \(q_x\) and \(q_y\) are the velocity components in the \(x\) and \(y\) directions. Equivalently,

\[q_x = \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial y}, \qquad q_y = \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial x}.\]

The streamlines are the level curves of \(\Psi\), while the equipotential curves are the level curves of \(\Phi\). Since the flow is incompressible and irrotational, these curves intersect orthogonally, and the velocity field is invariant under a conformal change of variables.

This is the key idea behind conformal mapping in fluid mechanics: a complicated flow domain can be mapped to a simpler one, such as the upper half-plane or the unit disk, where the complex potential is easier to write down.
